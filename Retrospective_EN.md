# Retrospective — Written to the Fabiana from Week 1

When I started this internship, I was not just looking for a new technical skill. I was looking for a way to prove — to myself first, and then to whoever might hire me — that I could take a project from nothing all the way to something finished, using a tool I had never worked with before. That was already familiar territory in a way: my Proof Statement was built on the Chile project, where I had to validate illegible street addresses street by street on Google Earth before technicians could be sent to install a network. What I have always cared about, in work and in life, is the same thing: what is real, what is true, what is coherent. I pay more attention to details than to what people say — how someone treats a waiter tells me more than their words do. At work, that translates into wanting to know exactly what needs to be done, why it needs to be done that way, and whether there is a better way to do it. My mind goes toward solutions, not toward dwelling on problems.

So when I came into this program, I expected to learn how to finish a real project with AI. What I did not expect was how much the experience would change my relationship with AI itself.

I started out believing I could trust AI completely. I was wrong, and the process of finding that out was the most important thing that happened these eight weeks. Working through the material, I noticed that without my own background knowledge, I had no way to tell whether what the AI told me was true, wrong, or simply invented. Adding to that, I don't speak English fluently, so I had to translate every assignment brief into Portuguese to study it, and then translate my work back into English with AI's help to publish it on FlyRank's platform. Early on, more than once, I read and reread a brief and still could not understand what was actually being asked — even after translating it. That is when I learned to use AI differently: not just to translate, but to help me interpret what a document was really asking for. That same instinct — read carefully, correct what doesn't make sense, but stay open when AI shows me a different angle worth considering — became a habit I now use without thinking about it.

Looking ahead, my next real case study will be the PI IV project at Univesp: predicting unfavorable weather conditions for free flight practice at Rampa Leste. But that project has a real limit I have to be honest about — it's a team of eight people, so the direction isn't mine alone to decide. What I do want to build on my own, to increase my chances at a real junior role, is a deeper technical foundation in machine learning. I genuinely did not expect to fall in love with working with AI the way I did. It challenges me, makes me question more, makes me look for reliable sources instead of just accepting an answer — and that pushes me to want to study more, not less.

If I had to narrow down what I'm taking with me into any future job, it comes down to three things. First, turning what's confusing into something clear — on both ends: using AI to decode instructions I couldn't fully understand even after translating them, and using that same instinct to organize messy technical material, like a spec and scattered test logs, into a README a stranger could actually follow. Second, refining in layers instead of starting over: a wrong first result doesn't mean try again from scratch, it means diagnosing which layer failed — objective, context, format, or verification — and fixing it one step at a time. Third, using AI as a thinking partner without outsourcing my judgment to it: letting it question my direction or offer another angle, but always coming back to the same question I've always asked myself — is this true, and does this actually hold up?

A resume can say I worked with data and with AI. It can't show that, faced with something ambiguous, I don't move forward until I've checked it myself. That's still the story this portfolio exists to tell.
